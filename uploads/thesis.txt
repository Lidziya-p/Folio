Abstract
People’s concern about their body image has never been
more serious, and yet the set ideals have never been so
unattainable.
Through research I discovered that shopping is the main body
dissatisfaction factor affecting women. Therefore I aimed at
not only providing women with a practical solution of effective
size search but also help them reduce their body
dissatisfaction.
My solution offers an alternative way of shopping by creating
location-based micro-communities of shoppers sharing
similar body traits and showing relevant reviews in real-time
context through augmented reality.
On psychological level it provides means of resisting shame by
practicing critical awareness, reaching out to other women
and speaking up about shame in the context of shopping.

1. Introduction
1.1 Background
Due to the vast influence of social media and dominance of
visual content across the majority of channels of
communication, people’s concern about their body image has
never been so serious, and yet the set ideals have never been
so unattainable.
According to Mental Health Foundation survey [1], carried out
in 2019 in the UK, only 21% of adults (23% of men and 19% of
women) felt satisfied with their body image, 20% (15% of men
and 25% of women) felt shame and 34% (25% of men and 43%
of women) felt low, and 19% (12% of men and 26% of women)
felt disgusted because of their body image. In Mail.Ru survey,
carried out in 2017 in Russia [2], 70% of women and 63% of men
admitted being dissatisfied with their body. Based on World
Health Organization data [3] published in 2015, 38% of 15-year-
old girls and 22% of boys in Estonia think that they are too fat,
although same statistics indicated that the real percentage of
overweight and obese people in this age group was 9%
among females and 19% among males [4].
Body dissatisfaction has become a global tendency, but
women have higher rates of body image concern, that can
result in serious mental health problems, such as depression,
anxiety, eating disorders and influence all spheres of a
person’s life.
1.2 Personal interest
For my thesis project, I got inspired by Naomi Wolf, who in her
book ‘Beauty Myth’ [5], explored the concept of beauty as a
social currency, and stated that ‘beauty myth and attainment
of beauty is a new virtue that is well suited for a new consumer
imperative and a new justification for economic unfairness in
the workplace. Somehow, somewhere, someone must have
figured out that women will buy more things if they are kept in
the self-hating, ever-failing, hungry, and sexually insecure
state of being aspiring “beauties.”

2. Design Approach
I followed the Double Diamond framework, developed by
Design Council in 2005. The two diamonds represent a process
of exploring an issue more widely or deeply
(divergent thinking) and then taking focused action
(convergent thinking)[6]. Using The Double Diamond, I
structured my project in four phases:
• Discover, where I started with the initial question of how to
build body confidence in women, looked for the problem to
solve by using the collage method, doing user interviews and
carrying out desk research.
• Define, where I made sense of my research by analyzing
user interviews, generating opportunities and formulating
How Might We Questions.
• Develop, where I looked for potential solutions by
brainstorming and sketching, evaluated my ideas and
picked a concept for further development.
• Deliver, where I created prototypes and tested them with
potential users.

3 Key definitions and focus
3.1 Body image
Body image is defined as the picture an individual forms in
their mind that constitutes beliefs, feelings, sensations,
behaviours and self-perception, concerning their own body
[7]. It also includes experiences of embodiment and
investment in appearance. Body dissatisfaction can be
defined as a negative evaluation of body size, shape, muscle
tone, weight, and it usually involves a perceived difference
between a person’s assessment of their body and their ideal
body.
3.2 Target audience
When defining my target audience for the project, I found out
that same body dissatisfaction concerns are assigned to
women from late teens till late 30s. And only middle-aged
women (40–49) were reported as having more realistic age-
based comparisons, being concerned to “look good for their
age,” rather than to look like women in magazine pictures or
on television, seeing body-related changes such as weight
gain as an inevitable[8]. That’s why I decided to see what
happens in the mid-period and design for women between 27
- 35, the age when women most likely have already graduated
from university and have less peer pressure, have a career or
family life, in other words, changed their lifestyle from the one
they had when they were younger.
3.3 Focus areas
To define my focus in this broad and complex topic, I have
reviewed existing models of body image formation and picked
the one that provided the most extensive overview and could
help me define what parts I could research and create impact
on as a designer.
Cognitive-behavioural model
According to cognitive-behavioural model [9], body image is
influenced by events from the past and present (figure 2).

Interpersonal influences manifest themselves in our relations
with people, especially peers, family or romantic partners.
Parents can influence the body image of their children by
commenting about a child’s weight or appearance,
introducing particular food rules or by setting an example
through dieting and overexercising.
Peers influence women through “fat talk” (discussion about
their and other people’s weight loss or weight gain),
comments and conversations about weight and shape, and
the belief that popularity is dependent on being thin, or having
an ‘idol’ to copy.
Cultural socialization implies that people learn from an early
age how society values different physical characteristics and
what meanings are assigned to them. For example, if we think
about what a superhero might look like, we will probably
imagine a muscular man.
Physical characteristics are connected with the fact that a
body is dynamic and changes over time. Even if puberty, facial
acne or extra weight were a matter of an individual’s past,
body image could still remember them. That’ why, for example,
people who lost weight might still feel fat.
Personality traits such as perfectionism or insecure
attachment patterns can be catalysts to body dissatisfaction.
Everyday situations and events trigger certain emotions,
thoughts and feelings, and some of them can be painful and
disturbing, and initiate a chain of responses. Based on those
events, an individual can formulate certain assumptions about
themselves, and starts performing certain actions, aimed at
appearance fixing.
All emotions, comments from people, assumptions make
foundation for schemas, which determine how an individual
perceives reality, thinks about events and themselves.
3.4 Thesis direction
Using the cognitive -behavioural model, I reviewed solutions
that are already suggested to people and found out that there
are solutions that:

4. User research
4.1 Collage Session
I decided to use the collage method as a starting point of my
research for three reasons. Firstly, it could provide me with a
way to narrow down without defining any specific questions.
Secondly, images are powerful triggers of unconscious
emotions and past experiences, and it could be at hand with
such a personal and very emotional topic as relations with the
body. Thirdly, it could help me understand how sensitive
women are about it and how careful I should be during
interviews.
For the collage session I invited 3 women matching my target
audience:
• 	Kate, school teacher, 32,
• 	Helen, 30, jewellery maker,
• 	Ann, 28, QA engineer.
I gave them different types of magazines, such as Vogue,
Cosmopolitan, Shape and National Geographic. I suggested to
tear out all the images they like and put them in a stack, and
only then start creating collages. Before the collage session, I
created my variant of collage to check how my ideas and
expectations align with others or how different they are. Also, it
could serve me as a reminder of confirmation biases all
people have (Figure 3) and stop me from taking a direction I
want to go instead of focusing on what women are
experiencing.
After collage session (Figure 4), I asked all women to tell about
their choice of images. Most women admitted that body
confidence has a lot to do with clothes, and my collage was
very different compared to theirs.
I singled out the themes, that can have further development
during interviews:
• clothes as a part of being body confident,
• expressing yourself and being brave and showing yourself to
the world,
• receiving attention from men.

4.2 User Interviews
I conducted eight interviews (Figure 5), of women matching
my age, profession and background requirements:
• 	age between 27 - 35
• 	university graduates
• 	having a career
I chose the form of an unstructured interview, and prepared
some questions in advance, but tried to keep it informal and
free-flowing, so that my interviewees could feel as if they are
having a normal conversation. I started with broader questions
and narrowed down when I saw a recurring pattern in
women’s stories.
Main goals of the interviews:
• find out what everyday situations and events trigger
negative emotions, and what emotions and thoughts women
experience during those events and situations,
• learn about coping techniques women worked out in
response to activating events,
• discover common values among women, that can be
supported in the development of my concept,
• see if themes discovered during the collage session have
any development in what women were telling.
4.3 Interview results
When I analyzed the interviews, I found out that women worked
out specific ways how to cope with body dissatisfaction
through fitness, finding people they feel support from, hobbies
or raising their critical awareness of the social phenomena
connected with body image.
Fitness is for mental health, not for ‘beach body’.
Women see fitness as a way of coping with body
dissatisfaction, and use it together with other practices to feel
better about themselves.
‘Yoga and other body practices help me a lot,

5. Desk Research
Interviews provided me with sufficient amount of evidence
that shopping for clothes is an everyday trigger for many
women, and it goes far beyond wasting time and not finding
the right size, but has a negative impact on emotional life. But
my research would be incomplete without seeing a bigger
picture behind collected insights. To see how the problem is
shaped by psychology, culture and retail industry I conducted
desk research and set the following aims:
• explore what industry factors make shopping for clothes
complex and not capable of catering for all women,
• review what characteristics of the retail industry have an
opportunity to be adjusted by my solution,
• discover why failed shopping experience creates so many
negative emotions from the point of view of culture and
psychology
• have an overview of existing solutions
• see how women challenge the situation and what solutions
they come up with
5.1 Retail industry overview
Fast change of collections
Zara offers 24 new clothing collections each year; H&M offers
12 to 16 and refreshes them weekly. Among all European
apparel companies, the average number of clothing
collections has more than doubled, from two a year in 2000 to
about five a year in 2011 [10]. This is aimed at ‘creating the
feeling that anything the customer could see at any given
moment as an article they might possibly buy would cease to
be available within a few days. [11]’
Usage of psychological mechanisms
Apart from scarcity, shops rely on coping mechanism called
“compensatory consumption.” This mechanism means that
even when a woman can only fit larger sizes at a specific
retailer, she’ll purchase other products, such as jewellery or
23

-- 23 of 88 --

make-up to boost her self-esteem. They also exploit ‘’self-
discrepancy gap’ - a difference between the way you see
yourself and the way you’d like to be. As April Lane Benson, a
psychologist who specializes in the treatment of compulsive
buying disorders, put it: ‘it nudges us to the world of fantasy, a
world where what we wish to happen takes precedence over
what we can reasonably expect to happen. Who you are
becomes confused with what you have’ [12]. Those two
phenomena manifest themselves in shopping as a way to
camouflage a negative body image and strive to look like
somebody else or make yourself a better version, instead of
accepting who you are.
Vanity Sizing
Today, clothing manufacturers are often using “vanity sizing,”
the labelling of clothes with sizes smaller than the actual cut of
the items. So, those size 34 slacks might really be closer to size
36, and perhaps even bigger [13]. (Figure 6). It is done both by
mass market and luxury brands to increase sales and is based
on shoppers’ desire to feel slim. Vanity sizing has a negative
side, resulting in smaller women being removed from standard
size range and introduction of 000 size of clothes and setting
an even more unattainable body ideal to those having body
dissatisfaction.

Size as a brand identity
Brand’s clothing shape, proportions of each size and eases
convey a brand’s philosophy and help a brand to stand out
from other brands. In other words, brands sell not only clothes
but also a certain lifestyle and a certain fashion body.
Absence of Standards
Throughout history, there were attempts to create a universal
size charts. For example, the US National Bureau of Standards
published “Body Measurements for the Sizing of Women’s
Patterns and Apparel” in 1958 based on the 15,000 women
interviews. Those standards were updated in 1970 and were
ditched in 1983, after being proven not to be realistic. The
reason why any standardization fails hides in our genetic
variety of sizes and proportions (Figure 7). As a result, modern
brands have to determine their size charts themselves.

5.2 Psychological and socio-cultural
factors of shopping and fashion
Being different while being the same
According to Mari Grinde Arntzen [14], fashion is run by two
human needs: stand out from the crowd and at the same time,
comply and be like others. On the one hand, we want to
demonstrate our personality and our individual features, but
on the other hand, we want to be a part of society or a group
of people. And every day we are balancing between those two
needs, and this balance is powered by fashion.
Fashion as communication
Clothes act as a means of self-identification, because they are
a way people interact with the world, transmit information
about themselves and let other people interpret it. Nowadays,
due to the overload of information and lack of time, people are
guided by instincts more and thus tend to judge people more
by their appearance. Otherwise, people would not be able to
manage in this world.
Fashion promotes comparison
‘Considering that the majority of social media and
advertisement is image-based, it becomes apparent that
social comparison could be a serious issue for its users’
mental health and well being’. [15], which results in negative
changes in self-esteem, body satisfaction and mental health.
Beauty as social must
Naked body by itself does not bear any valuable information.
Clothes serve as a body that we want to show to this world,
hide our flaws and display a self-made and constructed ideal.
This concept is especially evident in the fact that the fashion
system itself is aimed mostly at women. According to a theory
called ‘Beauty system’ [16], women are considered beautiful
and feminine based on the amount of effort they put in
themselves. That’s why women indulge themselves into
beauty practices and buy more: naturally looking and
unconstructed female appearance is not considered beautiful.
But because it is a false identity imposed on women, it creates
a conflict between what an individual wants to be and how
others see her, and results in experiencing shame.

Shame
Dr. Brené Brown, a research professor who has spent the past
two decades studying courage, vulnerability, shame, and
empathy, states that modern society does not talk about
shame, it is a ‘silent epidemic’ [17].
She describes it in a number of definitions:
• A feeling that you can not talk about because then everyone
would know your dirty little secret.
• Shame is being rejected
• You work hard to show the world what it wants to see, but
your mask falls and everyone sees unlikeable parts of you.
• Shame is feeling like an outsider - not belonging.
• Self-loathing
• Shame is like a prison, you deserve being in because
something is wrong with you.
• Shame is being exposed - the flawed parts of yourself that
you want to hide.

experience of believing we are flawed and as a result,

5.5 Desk research summary
Interviews followed by a desk research helped me see a bigger
picture behind an everyday shopping experience. I found out
that:
• from the point of view of technology, solutions reached a
high level of visualization and accuracy, but still are unable

run on retailer’s initiative and aimed mostly at online
shopping,
• from retail perspective, it is still impossible to adjust ready-
to-wear clothes to all figures and sizes and change
approach to sales in the near future: it is not only a complex
economic and logistics system, but also a system exploiting
psychology to drive sales.

emotionally painful experience for women, because it means
failing to conform to social norms and convey the right
message about themselves. Taking a bigger size or not
finding anything to wear starts the process of self-
comparison with media images and other people, resulting
in feeling of unworthiness, shame and being alone with those
kinds of problems.
• from people’s approach, creating groups and leaving
comments is the most common way how to share

shame resilience in a form of accepting vulnerability,

connection. The biggest drawback of this solution is that
findings are location and time based, and they lose their

6. Ideation
6.1 Design principles
Design principles are ‘the guardrails of your solution—quick,
memorable recipes that will help keep further iterations
consistent. These principles describe the most important
elements of solution and give integrity and form to what you’re
designing’ [19]. I formulated them based on my research.
Usage of the current paradigm
• My solution should not be aimed at redesign of retail system
or its parts. It means that I can not redesign sizes creation
because it can become one more means of psychologically
nudging people into buying and lead to the creation of even
more insecurities in women
• My solution can take advantage of shops organization, for
example, how clothes are displayed or how often they
change collections.
Promotion of positive comparison
• My solution should aim at normalization of female bodies,
showing real people with real bodies in real situations and
support values common to my target group, described in
user interviews results.
Building a sense of community
• The solution should incorporate principles of shame
resilience, formulated by Brene Brown, such as practicing
mutual support, sharing experiences, this way creating a
feeling of everyone ‘being in the same boat’.
Real-time contextual information
• Solution should provide relevant information during
shopping.
Non Intrusiveness
• Solution should not eliminate the pleasurable parts of
shopping experience, but rather be an assistant, usage of
which is initiated by users.

Popularity of Augmented Reality
AR technology has become democratized after introduction of
Snapchat’s Lense Studio and Facebook’s Spark AR Studio,
allowing everyone without developer skills design, build and

and their online presence and increased involvement and
interest of users in Augmented Reality. For example, in the first
year (2017) of Lens Studio, creators have submitted more than
250,000 Lenses that have been viewed more than 15 billion
times [24].
Current state of technology and it affordance
Apple’s ARKit and Google’s ARCore software development kits
(SDKs) were introduced in 2017 and have standardized the
development tools, making mobile AR app creation accessible
to the mass audience.
Latest Apple’s ARKit 3 can [25]:
• naturally show AR content in front of or behind people using
People Occlusion,
• track up to three faces at a time,
• support collaborative sessions,
• simultaneously use face and world tracking on the front and
back cameras,
• capture motion,
• detect up to 100 images at a time,
• get an automatic estimate of the physical size of the images,
• recognize objects in complex environments
• use machine learning to detect planes in the environment
faster.

Hardware
Smartphones have already become powerful devices, capable
of supporting AR experience, and over upcoming years will
acquire new features and sensors. For example, Time-of-flight

8.2 Psychology behind the solution
If we have a look at the app from Shame Resilience Theory,
we can see that my solution provides several ways to build
shame resilience.
My whole solution is aimed at shopping as a body
dissatisfaction trigger, and because of its purpose it already
serves as a way of acknowledging personal vulnerability. In
other words, if a woman downloads an app, it is already an
unconscious first step to addressing her trigger.
On the level of practicing critical awareness, the solution does
not impose an ideal of how a woman should look, but brings
out real people, usually invisible in media. By showing real
women it creates a way to normalize real bodies in real
situations. It provides a bigger context for feeling shame while
shopping for clothes. As Brene Brown says, ‘shame works only if
we are alone in it. If we think there is someone else, a group of
women, a world full of women, struggling with the same issue,
the concept of shame becomes bankrupt.’
Reaching out manifests itself in the solution in the form of
reviews and reactions, which are a way to share a personal
story within a context of shopping.

9. Reflections
9.1 Process reflection
In my opinion, carrying out this thesis project has given a boost
to me as a designer and added valuable skills and attitudes to
my future work.
Firstly, I managed to find efficient strategies of narrowing down
and finding the right focus through choosing the right
methods at the right time. It was especially important at the
beginning, because the topic of body confidence is complex
and consists of multiple levels.
Secondly, I fully achieved my personal goals, stated in the
beginning of the project: improved my skills of interviewing
people and analyzing interviews and got new skills in user
testing with professional tools.
Thirdly, I overcame my personal biases and underestimating
the power of research. Before the project it felt to me that it
means researching all the creativity out of design, but now I
can see the value of it and, what is more important, approach
more critically my work and works of others, and see and
evaluate the amount of research applied in other people’s
solutions.
Lastly, I mastered my time planning skills and learned a
valuable lesson of meeting deadlines.
9.2 Future development
Despite reaching my goals for this project, I would like to refine
my solution further. I plan to test my solution in a set up similar
to the shop environment, which was impossible because of
coronavirus. Although I tested functions of my solution and got
feedback on it, I feel that testing in real environment can give a
better understanding of psychology behind the solution. How
women would feel if they saw their body twins? Would it be
uncomfortable? Would their attitude to their body change over
time?
In addition to that, I would like to scale my solution to AR
glasses and see how change of the device will influence
interaction. Probably lot’s of interactions will happen just by

9.3 Solution within a context of bigger
trends
Shopping after corona
Lifestyle and shopping will change after coronavirus, especially
in-store shopping, which my solution is aimed at. On the one
hand, Report by the Business of Fashion (BoF) and consultants
McKinsey products states that global fashion sales are
predicted to fall by up to 30% in 2020, and the luxury end of the
market will be even harder hit, with sales slumping by up to
40%[28]. It also predicts that social distancing has already
made digital an urgent priority. On the other hand, people do
not always act logically, and we can not fully predict their
behaviour. For example, Hermes's flagship store in
Guangzhou recorded US$2.7 million in sales (S$3.8 million) just
a day after reopening [29], due to "revenge spending" (a
buying binge by shoppers emerging from lockdown). My
solution can still be viable within both scenarios: either if
women are happy and enjoy coming back to normal life more
than ever, or if they need a way to minimize time spent in
crowded places and a tool for faster decisions making when
shopping.
Search for meaningful connection
GlobalWebIndex's Consumer Trends for 2019 report suggests
that stepping away or downsizing on social media has
become an increasing trend throughout the past year.
According to, Reddit Co-founder Alexis Ohanian, people want
to belong to a smaller community, where they can be heard
and where they can meet like-minded people. "What people
seem to be clamoring for more and more now is community,
and so whether that goes to private group chats — like your
WhatsApp group, your Signal group — or new platforms that
have emerged," he said [30]. My solution can provide an active

experience around the subject of interest for people interested
in fashion and those who need help and support.
Overcoming insecurities
Size and body shape is a topic widely discussed in media and
among people, but rarely spoken out loud in a context of
everyday activities. As one of the women I interviewed said: 'I
like sauna, but I don't go there. Everyone around me will be slim
and sporty, and I have a fat belly.' People think about it and
avoid many pleasurable activities, where their body will be
exposed. I believe my solution can be implemented in different
ways: help people overcome their insecurities by organizing
interest-based groups: go together to the sauna, start working
out with people like you or organize an efficient clothes swap
party and be sure that everyone will find something they like.
Feminism
I started my thesis with a quote by Naomi Wolf, and would like
to finish it with another Naomi Wolf’s quote: ‘Ironically, the myth
that drives women apart also binds them together.
Commiserating about the myth is as good as a baby to bring
strange women into pleasant contact, and break down the line
of Other Woman wariness. A wry smile about calories, a
complaint about one’s hair, can evaporate the sullen
examination of a rival in the fluorescent light of a ladies’ room.
On one hand, women are trained to be competitors against all
others for “beauty”; on the other, when one woman—a bride, a
shopper in a boutique—needs to be adorned for a big
occasion, other women swoop and bustle around her in
generous concentration in a team formation as effortlessly
choreographed as a football play. These sweet and satisfying
rituals of being all on the same side, these all-too-infrequent
celebrations of shared femaleness, are some of the few
shared female rituals left; hence their loveliness and power.
But, sadly, these delightful bonds too often dissolve when the
women reenter public space and resume their isolated,
unequal, mutually threatening, jealously guarded “beauty”
status.’ I believe that from feminist perspective my solution
can be a digital way of practicing female culture and create
invisible bonds and solidarity between women.
